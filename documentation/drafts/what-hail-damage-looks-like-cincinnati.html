Hail Damage · From My Inspections

What hail actually does to a roof.
Photos from my inspections.

By Joe Deal July 2026 7 min read

Most hail damage doesn't look like damage. It looks like nothing — until you know what you're looking for. Here's what I actually find on Cincinnati roofs, with photos from my own inspections.

The last hailstorm I worked was [JO: storm date] in [JO: town/neighborhood]. The homeowner met me in the driveway and told me his roof was fine — he'd walked around the house, looked up, didn't see a single missing shingle. Forty-five minutes later I came down the ladder with [JO: what you actually found — e.g. "photos of two dozen bruised shingles and a dented ridge vent"].

That gap — between what a roof looks like from the ground and what's actually happening up on the shingles — is the whole reason I'm writing this. Hail damage almost never looks dramatic. There's no gaping hole, no shingles in the yard. What there is, is a roof that's quietly ten years closer to failing than it was the day before the storm, and an insurance claim window that's already ticking.

So here's what hail damage actually looks like. Real photos, from my inspections, on roofs around Greater Cincinnati. No stock images of golf-ball hail sitting in someone's palm.

What Hail Bruising Actually Is

When a hailstone hits an asphalt shingle, two things happen. First, it knocks the granules loose at the point of impact — those little ceramic-coated rocks that protect the asphalt underneath from UV. Second, if the hit is hard enough, it fractures the fiberglass mat inside the shingle. Roofers call that a bruise, and it's the right word: like a bruise on an apple, the surface can look almost normal while the structure underneath is broken.

A bruised shingle doesn't leak the next day. It leaks in two or three years, after freeze-thaw cycles and summer heat work that fracture open and the exposed asphalt dries out and cracks. By then, most homeowners have no idea the leak traces back to a storm — and the claim window on that storm has usually closed. That's why I tell people: the damage is real on day one, even when the leak isn't.

Hail vs. Blisters vs. Foot Traffic — How I Tell the Difference

This is the part that separates a real inspection from a guy pointing at spots. Not every mark on a shingle is hail, and if I call blistering "hail damage" on a report, I torch my credibility with every adjuster in this market — and I torch your claim with it. So here's how I actually tell them apart.

Hail is random. Random size, random spacing, random locations. A storm doesn't hit every third shingle in a neat line. And hail hits the whole property — if I see bruising on the shingles but the gutters, downspouts, window capping, and AC fins are all clean, I get skeptical. Soft metals dent at lower impact energy than shingles bruise. Real hail almost always signs its name on the metal first.

Blisters are manufacturing, not weather. A blister is a spot where gas got trapped in the asphalt when the shingle was made, then popped in the heat. Blisters are raised around the edges — like a little crater with a rim — where a hail bruise is pushed in. Blisters also show up scattered on slopes that hail couldn't have favored, and there's no fractured mat underneath. Press on a blister pop and it's solid. Press on a bruise and it gives.

Foot traffic follows paths. Scuffed granules from someone walking the roof — a gutter cleaner, a satellite installer, a previous "inspector" — run in lines from the ladder point to wherever they were headed. Hail doesn't walk routes. If the damage maps to a path, it's boots, not weather.

"If a contractor circles every mark on your roof and calls it all hail, that's not an inspection. That's a sales pitch with chalk."

There's also mechanical damage from the hail claim gold rush itself — and yes, that's a real thing in this market. I've been on roofs where the "hail hits" were suspiciously round, suspiciously uniform, and suspiciously fresh compared to the storm date. Adjusters know what fabricated damage looks like, and one fake hit can get an entire legitimate claim denied. It's one more reason to have someone honest on the roof before anyone else.

The One-Inch Myth

You'll hear this one a lot, sometimes from adjusters: "Hail under an inch can't damage a roof." It's a rule of thumb that's been repeated so often people treat it like physics. It isn't.

Whether a hailstone bruises a shingle depends on more than its diameter. It depends on how fast it was moving, whether wind was driving it at an angle, how dense the stone was — and most of all, the condition of the shingle it hit. A three-year-old architectural shingle is flexible and padded with granules; it can shrug off hits that would wreck an older roof. A 15-year-old 3-tab that's been baking on a south-facing slope for a decade and a half is brittle. Smaller stones bruise it. I've documented qualifying damage on aging roofs from storms where the biggest reported hail was under that magic one-inch line — and I've walked roofs after big-hail events that genuinely didn't need a claim.

The honest version of the rule is this: hail size tells you whether to get the roof looked at, not whether it's damaged. The only way to know what actually happened is to put someone on the shingles. Which brings me to the practical part.

Questions I Get After Every Hailstorm

My roof is only a few years old. Can it still have hail damage?

Yes, though it takes a harder hit. Newer shingles are more flexible and better granule-protected, so they resist smaller hail — but a serious storm bruises new roofs too. Age changes the threshold, not the physics.

My neighbor got a new roof from this storm. Does that mean I will?

It means you should get inspected — same storm, same street is a strong signal. But it's not a guarantee. Slope direction, shingle age, and tree cover change what each roof took. I've approved-and-replaced one side of a street while the other side genuinely didn't qualify.

Does hail damage get worse if I wait?

The bruise itself doesn't spread, but the consequences do: freeze-thaw works the fractures open, granule loss accelerates, and eventually you've got exposed asphalt and leaks. And separately from the roof — your window to file the claim is limited by your policy. Waiting costs you on both clocks.
