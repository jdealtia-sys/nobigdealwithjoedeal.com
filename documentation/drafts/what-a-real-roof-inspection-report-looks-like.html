The roof inspection report
I give every homeowner.

By Joe Deal July 2026 8 min read

Most "free roof inspections" are ten-minute sales visits with a clipboard prop. Here's the actual report I hand you at the end of mine — a real one, redacted, walked through page by page.

Here's a test you can run on any roofer who offers you a free inspection: ask what you get to keep when it's over. If the answer is a handshake and a price, that wasn't an inspection. That was a sales call that happened to involve a ladder.

I've followed behind enough of those visits to know how they go. Ten minutes on the roof — sometimes not even that, sometimes binoculars from the driveway — then a lot of urgency at your kitchen table. No photos you can look at. No measurements. No document. Just "trust me, it's bad" and a contract with today's date on it.

I do it differently, and the difference is a document. Every inspection I do ends with a written, photographed report that's yours to keep whether you ever spend a dollar with me or not. Give it to your insurance company. Give it to another contractor for a second bid. Stick it in a drawer. It's yours. Below is what's actually in it — a real report from a real house, with the customer's info blacked out.

Why the Report Is the Whole Point

A roof inspection without a report is just an opinion, and opinions are worth what you paid for them. The report is what turns 45 minutes on your shingles into something you can act on: compare bids against it, hand it to an adjuster, or file it away as a baseline so that when a storm rolls through two years from now, there's dated proof of what your roof looked like before.

That last one matters more than people realize. One of the most common ways hail claims go sideways is the carrier arguing the damage is old. A dated inspection report from before the storm ends that argument before it starts.

"If the inspection is free but there's no report, you're not the customer. You're the lead."

The Report, Page by Page

Page 1 — The property and the roof, on paper

First page is the boring stuff that turns out to matter: the address, the inspection date, the weather that day, and the basics of the roof itself — shingle type, approximate age [JO: confirm what your cover page actually lists], number of layers, and the overview shots. This is the page that makes the report usable as evidence later. An undated stack of photos proves nothing; a dated document with the roof's baseline condition proves a lot.

Pages 2–3 — Photo documentation, slope by slope

Then the photos — and not four of them. I photograph every slope, every penetration (pipe boots, vents, chimney), the valleys, the ridge, the flashing, and the gutters, plus close-ups of anything wrong. Every photo is labeled with where it was taken, because "damaged shingle" means nothing and "hail bruise, south slope, third course above the gutter line" means something an adjuster can go verify.

When I find damage, you get the wide shot and the close-up. The wide shot proves where it is. The close-up proves what it is. Adjusters are trained to distrust close-ups without context — a zoomed-in photo of a cracked shingle could be from any roof in Ohio. Wide-plus-close is how you document a roof so the claim doesn't come down to trust.

Page 4 — Measurements and the condition rundown

The report includes the roof's measurements — total squares, pitch, ridge and valley lengths [JO: confirm which measurements your report includes and how you capture them]. Nothing gets skipped just because it looked fine from the ladder.

Last page — What I'd actually do, and what I wouldn't

The report ends with a recommendation in plain English: repair this, watch that, replace nothing — or yes, this one's a replacement, and here's why. If insurance is in play, I say so. If it isn't, I say that too. And plenty of my reports end with "your roof is fine, see you in a few years" — because if the recommendation is always "replace," it's not a recommendation, it's a script.

Red Flags vs. Yellow Flags

People ask me how to read their own report, so here's the short version of how I triage what I find.

Red Flags — Deal With It Now

  • Exposed nail heads or fastener pops backing out through the shingle — every one is a future leak with a schedule.
  • Cracked or rotted pipe boots — the single most common leak source I find, and one of the cheapest fixes if you catch it early.
  • Flashing pulling away from the chimney or walls — water is already finding that gap.
  • Soft decking underfoot — the leak already happened; now we're talking about how much wood it took with it.
  • Active interior staining that's damp or growing — that's not a roof problem anymore, it's a house problem.

Yellow Flags — Watch, Don't Panic

  • Even granule wear consistent with the roof's age — aging isn't damage.
  • A few scattered blisters — cosmetic, usually manufacturing, worth photographing so nobody calls them hail later.
  • Slight curling on the oldest slope — a sign to start budgeting, not an emergency.
  • Moss or algae staining — ugly before it's harmful; a soft wash handles it.

A contractor who calls every yellow flag a red flag is selling. A report that separates the two is the paper trail of somebody telling you the truth.

How an Insurance Adjuster Reads the Same Report

When there's a claim involved, this report does double duty — because the adjuster and I are looking at the same roof through different software. They work a checklist: is there a qualifying event, does the damage pattern match it, how many hits per test square, what's the scope. My report is built so their checklist can be answered straight off my pages — labeled photos, slope-by-slope documentation, measurements that match their math.

That does two things for you. It makes the adjuster's job easy when the claim is legitimate, which speeds everything up. And if their estimate comes in low or their scope misses things, my report is the line-item evidence the supplement gets built from — not a phone call saying "the number feels small," but photographs and measurements pointing at exactly what got missed. Quick Answers

How long does your inspection take?

About 45 minutes to an hour on the roof and in the attic when there's access, plus write-up time. If someone inspected your roof in ten minutes, they inspected the ladder.

What does it cost?

Nothing. The report is yours either way. I earn work by being the guy whose document holds up — not by charging for the look.

Do I have to be home?

For the roof, no. For the attic check and to walk you through what I found, it helps. Either way you get the full report, not a summary.

Will you inspect before I buy or sell a house?

Yes — pre-purchase and pre-listing roof inspections are some of the most useful ones I do. Knowing the roof's real condition before the negotiation beats finding out after.
